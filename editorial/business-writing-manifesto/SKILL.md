A synthesis of principles drawn from 20 master writers, filtered through the lens of business utility.


## The Core Premise

Business writing exists for one reason: to change what someone thinks or does. Every sentence that fails this test is dead weight. This manifesto is built on that foundation.

---

## Principle 1: The Two-Tier Value Test

**Source:** Tim Ferriss's Input/Output Asymmetry + Your Reflection

Ferriss says: lower the bar to start, raise the bar to ship. But what *is* the bar for shipping?

Your insight cuts to the bone: **there are only two valid outcomes for a reader.**

**Tier 1:** They learned something genuinely new.

**Tier 2:** They already knew it conceptually, but now they have the actionable steps to implement it.

That's it. Everything else is fluff.

This creates a brutal editing filter. Before every paragraph survives, it must answer: *Does this teach something new, or does this make something actionable?* If neither, delete it.

**The Implication for Process:**

Write loose. Edit ruthless. The "two crappy pages" approach works for drafts — momentum matters. But when you ship, every sentence must pass the two-tier test. Mediocrity is not an option because mediocrity follows you. A mediocre piece is worse than no piece at all.

## Principle 2: Distinctiveness is a Thought Problem, Quality is a Craft Problem

**Source:** Kevin Kelly's "Be The Only" + Your Reflection

Kelly says don't compete on "better" — compete on "only." But you've identified the mechanism:

- **Distinctiveness comes from the thoughts themselves.** The raw ideas, the angles, the connections no one else is making.
- **Quality comes from the hardness.** The craft. The delivery. How you forge those thoughts into something transmissible.

You need both. Distinctive thoughts with poor craft = wasted insight. Beautiful craft with common thoughts = forgettable polish.

**The Business Writing Application:**

Most business writing fails on distinctiveness. People write what everyone else writes because they read what everyone else reads. The ideas are borrowed, the angles are stale, the insights are consensus.

The goal: surface things people are unaware of, or things they've ignored. Not "new" in the sense of invented — but new to *their attention*. Bring forward what has been overlooked. Flesh out what has been vague.

Then — and only then — does craft matter. The hardness. The delivery vehicle. This is where tightness comes in, where every word earns its place.

## Principle 3: The Barbell Information Diet (Extended)

**Source:** Cultural Tutor + Marc Andreessen + Your Reflection

The principle: consume only the timeless or the super-current. Ignore the mushy middle (yesterday to 10 years ago).

Your extension adds the business lens:

**The Timeless Side:**

- Fundamental metrics: CAC, LTV, ROAS, opportunity cost
- Fundamental frameworks: unit economics, competitive positioning, leverage
- These never shift. You can build on top of them indefinitely.

**The Super-Current Side:**

- Must be *actually current* — not last year's news dressed up
- Must be *actually applicable* — not theoretical future-state
- Must be in a domain where the edge matters (AI in coding = yes; AI in video editing = not yet)

**The Critical Nuance You Identified:**

The edge is always shifting. The timeless is stable ground. When you write about the edge, you're writing on sand — it will move.

The solution: **have an eye for trajectory.** You need enough observation time (a year of close monitoring) to understand not just where something *is*, but where it's *going*. Then you write about the trajectory, not just the snapshot.

This requires: knowledge (for the timeless) + intelligence + foresight (for the current).

**What This Means for Business Writing:**

Don't write about GPT capabilities in general — that's the mushy middle now. Either write about the unchanging principles of how to evaluate any tool (timeless), or write about what shipped *this week* and how to use it *today* (current).

The middle ground is noise. It's where most business content lives. Avoid it.

## Principle 4: Writing as Feeling Engineering

**Source:** Riva Tez's Word Painting + Your Reflection

Writing isn't just information transfer. It's mood creation. Word painting.

Your insight: **every paragraph must be judged in terms of feelings.**

This doesn't contradict the two-tier value test — it adds a layer. The *what* is value (new knowledge or actionable steps). The *how* is feeling.

The same information delivered with different craft produces different feelings. And feelings determine what people *do* with the information.

Consider:

- Does this paragraph create urgency or calm?
- Does it create confidence or doubt?
- Does it create curiosity or satisfaction?

The hardness — the craft — is what controls this. Two writers can communicate the same insight. One makes you want to act immediately. The other makes you nod and forget.

**The Business Writing Application:**

Business writing is not exempt from emotional engineering. The myth that "business = rational" is exactly that — a myth. Decisions are emotional. Action is emotional.

Write to create the feeling that produces the action you want.

## Principle 5: Vertical Excavation Beyond the Reader

**Source:** Ava's Vertical vs. Horizontal Thinking + Your Reflection

Writing forces vertical thinking. Thinking alone is horizontal — it skips, it jumps, it grazes surfaces. Writing chains you to a single idea and forces you down.

Your extension is the key: **your readers have been digging too.**

The people you're writing for — in business contexts — are not blank slates. They've thought about these problems. Some of them have thought about these problems for years. They've been digging at the same spot.

To deliver value, you must dig deeper than they have. Not one step further — ten steps further.

This is the real bar for business writing that matters. You're not writing for people who know nothing. You're writing for people who know *a lot* — and you need to show them something below the depth they've reached.

**How to Actually Do This:**

1. Identify consensus. What does everyone already agree on about this topic?
2. Go past consensus. What's below that? What's the second-order effect? What's the uncomfortable implication?
3. Keep going. What happens if you follow that thread even further?
4. Stop only when you hit something that surprises *you*.

If it doesn't surprise you, it won't surprise them. The gold is below your own comfort level.

## Principle 6: Alignment as Sustainability

**Source:** Tyler Cowen & Alex Tabarrok + Your Acknowledgment

Motion without force. Head, heart, wallet aligned.

You noted this "makes sense" and needs no elaboration. But it's worth stating explicitly in a manifesto:

**Writing you hate is writing you'll abandon.**

Business writing at scale — content marketing, thought leadership, whatever you call it — requires sustained output. Discipline alone doesn't sustain. Alignment does.

Write at the intersection of:

- What you're intellectually capable of exploring (head)
- What you're genuinely curious about (heart)
- What creates value in your market (wallet)

If any leg is missing, the stool falls over.

## Principle 7: Respect as Foundation

**Source:** Steven Pressfield + Your Acknowledgment

You said this needs no commentary. But it's the foundation under everything else.

If you don't respect the craft — if you treat writing as a necessary chore, a box to check, a marketing task to delegate — the writing will reflect that.

The reader can feel it. Disrespect leaks through.

## The Three Laws of Tightness

**Source:** Your additional principles

You mentioned writing must be "very tight," with "no fluff," and "not magazine-like."

This crystallizes into three laws:

**Law 1: No Sentence Without Purpose**
Every sentence either teaches something new, makes something actionable, or creates a necessary feeling. If it does none of these, cut it.

**Law 2: No Padding for Length**
Magazine writing pads for word count. Business writing has no word count. Say it in 100 words or 1000 — whatever the idea requires. But not one word more.

**Law 3: No Performance of Intelligence**
Fluff often comes from trying to *sound* smart rather than *be* useful. Complex vocabulary where simple works. Lengthy explanations where brevity serves. Hedging and qualifying to seem thorough.

Cut the performance. Deliver the value. Get out.

## Synthesis: The Operating System

Putting it all together, here is the operating system for business writing that matters:

**Before You Write:**

1. Identify your angle. Is it distinctive? Are you the only one saying this, or joining the chorus?
2. Check your inputs. Are you drawing from the timeless or the truly current? Or are you regurgitating the mushy middle?
3. Find your depth. Have you gone past consensus? Past your own comfort? Down to something that surprises you?

**While You Write:**

1. Write loose. Get the ideas down. Don't edit while drafting.
2. Think in feelings. What should each section make them feel?
3. Go vertical. When you want to skip to the next idea, don't. Dig deeper instead.

**Before You Ship:**

1. Apply the two-tier test. Every paragraph: new knowledge or actionable steps. If neither, cut.
2. Enforce tightness. No sentence without purpose. No padding. No performance.
3. Raise the bar. Would you stake your reputation on this for the next decade? If not, it's not ready.

---

## The Final Filter

Before anything goes out, ask one question:

**Would someone who has been thinking about this problem for five years learn something or be able to do something they couldn't before?**

If yes, ship.

If no, dig deeper or delete.

That's the whole game.
