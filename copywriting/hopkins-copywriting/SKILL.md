# Hopkins Copywriting

Claude Hopkins spent five decades and 16-hour days learning what makes people buy. His conclusions were few, and he repeated them until they were impossible to forget. His employer Albert Lasker locked the manuscript of *Scientific Advertising* in a safe for twenty years because it gave away everything. This skill turns those conclusions into a working method for any writing whose job is to be read and acted on: an ad, a landing page, an email, a headline, an article.

## The one test

Before and after writing anything, ask:

> **Would this help a salesman sell if the buyer were standing in front of him?**

Advertising is salesmanship in print, multiplied. A mediocre salesman hurts a slice of the business; mediocre copy hurts all of it. Every question about the writing (how long, what tone, what to lead with, what to cut) is answered by the salesman's standard. You would not gag a salesman at 50 words. You would not let him open with "we are the industry leader." You would not let him address a crowd when one person is asking a question.

## The workflow

Do these in order. Skipping research is the most common cause of weak copy, and the writer rarely notices because the prose still sounds fine.

### 1. Research until you find the keystone fact

Hopkins read volumes of material "dry as dust" to find the one line that carried a campaign. Ogilvy found "at 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock" in an engineering document nobody meant to publish. Behind a simple ad lie weeks of work. "Genius is the art of taking pains."

Ask for, or dig through:
- **How it's made, tested, sourced, delivered.** The maker sees only the ordinary here. Every "well, obviously we..." is a candidate.
- **Numbers.** Prices, margins, timings, counts, test results, years, distances, rejection rates.
- **Who buys and what they say.** Reviews, support tickets, sales calls, comments, refusals. Stay as close to the end buyer as you can get.
- **What competitors claim,** so you can find what is true, common, and never said.

If the user hasn't given you this material, ask for it, or state plainly which specifics are missing and mark placeholders like `[X%]`. **Never invent a statistic, process detail, testimonial, or claim.** A fabricated specific is worse than a vague one: it's a lie the reader can catch.

### 2. Find the appeal

The appeal is the one thing the reader gets. Not the feature, not the company, not the category. It answers the reader's silent question: *what does this do for me?*

- **Reward over prevention.** People will do almost anything to cure a trouble and little to prevent one. Sell the beauty, the speed, the money, the relief, the status. Do not picture the afflicted.
- **Specific over general.** "Lowest prices" claims the expected. "Our net profit is 3%" gets believed.
- **True and untold.** The commonplace fact told first becomes yours forever.
- **Not your taste.** What you like may appeal to a minority. Pick what a buyer, not a founder, would care about. Test when you can.

### 3. Write the headline: many candidates, pick one

The headline's job is to pick the right readers out of a crowd. "Hey there, Bill Jones." Nobody reads the whole newspaper; they choose by headline. Hopkins spent hours on a single headline and threw away scores. A headline change commonly multiplied returns five to ten times; Ogilvy saw nineteen. You cannot save souls in an empty church.

- Write **at least 15–20 candidates.** The first five are almost always about the product or company. Keep going until they're about the reader.
- Put the **main appeal in the headline.** A reader who reads nothing else should know what they'd get.
- **Name the reader or their situation** when you can.
- **Truth test:** the body must deliver what the headline promised.
- Present the strongest 3–5 to the user and say which you'd run and why.

Titles on articles, clips, videos, and posts are headlines. Distill the single idea the piece proves, not the topic it covers.

### 4. Write the body as a conversation with one person

Picture one typical buyer who read your headline, is half-sold, and is standing in front of you seeking information. Give them enough to act.

- **Lead with service.** The best ads ask no one to buy. They offer wanted information and cite advantages; buying is the natural result.
- **The more you tell, the more you sell.** Length is not the enemy; irrelevance is. Give the full case: reasons, process, proof, numbers, objections answered.
- **Tell the pains you take.** Give "quality" a concrete meaning.
- **Be specific everywhere.** Replace every adjective of degree with a number, comparison, or concrete image.
- **Give it a face.** People love people, not corporations. Let the real founder or maker speak in first person.
- **End with an easy action** that asks for a small effort (reply, sign up, request the sample). What's handed out unasked loses respect; what's requested gets valued.

### 5. Cut the selfishness

Read the draft once looking only for the writer's interests. Hopkins saw many an ad killed by a single selfish phrase. Delete or convert:
- "Buy now," "insist on our brand," "avoid imitations," "don't miss out," "limited time" (unless the limit is real and explained)
- "Industry-leading," "world-class," "award-winning," "we're proud to," "our mission is"
- Company size, age, or plant, unless converted into a benefit
- "We" as the subject of sentences that should have "you"

### 6. Check, then stick with what works

Run the self-check at the bottom. Once something works, don't change it for novelty. You're bored because you read every ad; the reader is seeing it for the first time. You aren't advertising to a standing army; you're advertising to a moving parade.

## The principles, with their stories

Use these when a user asks *why*, wants examples to cite, or needs convincing.

**Salesmanship in print.** Hopkins' whole framework rests on one substitution: an ad is a salesman, multiplied. Should copy be short? "Would you confine a salesman to a certain number of words with a prospect before him? That would be an unthinkable handicap."

**One person, not the mass.** A woman wrote asking about a sewing machine. The firm's habit was to mail every inquirer the same giant general catalog. Hopkins sent her the sewing-machine catalog with every style and price, plus the names of people in her own town who had bought one, with an invitation to go see the machines and ask the owners. "Treat people in advertising as we treat them in person. However big your business, get down to the units, for those units are all that make size."

**Service, never selfishness.** A brush maker's 2,000 door-to-door salesmen never asked anyone to buy. They said: "I was sent here to give you a brush. Take your choice." Choosing, she saw others she wanted, and felt the pull to reciprocate. The salesman left with an order. "Argue anything for your own advantage and people will resist to the limit. But seem unselfishly to consider your customers' desires and they will naturally flock to you." The two great faults of advertising: boasts and selfishness.

**The headline picks the reader.** "People are hurried. They want to be amused or benefited. There may be products which interest them more than anything else, but they will never know it unless the headline tells them." Modern proof: "1,000 songs in your pocket." Five words, the whole value proposition, and you want the rest.

**Truth told in a fascinating way.** John E. Powers was called by a clothier on the edge of failure. His prescription: tell the truth or I quit. The ad said, roughly: *We are bankrupt. We owe $125,000 more than we can pay. This announcement will bring our creditors down on us, but if you come and buy tomorrow we'll have the money to meet them. Here are the prices.* Truth was so rare it caused a sensation. Thousands came. The store was saved.

**Tell the pains you take.** Schlitz was fifth in beer. Every brewer shouted "pure." Hopkins toured the brewery: plate-glass rooms where beer cooled in filtered air, white-wood-pulp filters, pumps cleaned twice daily, bottles washed four times, wells sunk 4,000 feet, six months of aging, a mother yeast cell refined through 1,200 experiments. He asked why they'd never told anyone. *Every brewer does this.* His reply: "Others have never told this story. It amazes everyone who goes through your brewery. It will startle everyone in print." Schlitz went from fifth to neck-and-neck with first in months. "The maker is too close to his product. He sees in his methods only the ordinary."

**Be specific.** "Platitudes and generalities roll off the human understanding like water from a duck." Every clothier claimed the lowest prices; one said "my net profit is 3%" and it landed. Every razor claimed a quick shave; one advertised a 78-second shave, indicating an actual test, and sales rose sensationally. "The weight of an argument may often be multiplied by making it specific."

**The more you tell, the more you sell.** "It has never failed to prove out in any test we know." The interested reader wants the full case; brevity, for them, is withholding.

**Reward, not prevention.** Every toothpaste sold "prevent decay." Hopkins sold Pepsodent as a creator of beauty, never pictured the afflicted, showed only attractive people with beautiful teeth, and made a million dollars on it. He also bought $13,000 of the company's stock; it paid $200,000 in dividends and sold for $500,000. "People will do anything to cure a trouble, but little to prevent it."

**Human nature is fixed.** The same today as in Caesar's time; what you learn about it you never unlearn. Levers Hopkins named: *curiosity* (one of the strongest incentives; always pay it off); *price as signal* (a painting is ignored until people hear it cost $750,000; then a crowd forms); *belonging* (an offer limited to veterans, lodge members, or executives outperforms the same offer to everyone); *reciprocity* (the gift creates the wish to return it); *effort creates value*; and *coax, don't drive* ("whatever they do, they do to please themselves"). These describe how people decide. They are not substitutes for a good product or a true claim.

**Demonstration beats argument.** At ten, selling his widowed mother's homemade silver polish door to door, Hopkins sold to one woman in ten by talking and to nearly all when he could get inside and demonstrate. "A good product is its own best salesman." But sampling done wrong fails: "products handed out without asking lose respect. It is different when you force people to make an effort." Arouse interest first, then require a small request, then give generously. Estée Lauder built an empire the same way.

**Test; distrust your taste.** "We must never judge humanity by ourselves." Losses from personal preference "would easily pay the national debt." Hopkins owned an ocean-going yacht and asked whether anyone thought he crossed the ocean without a chart and compass. Products he thought stupid sold enormously; the world is bigger than one man's taste.

**Give it a person.** "Personalities appeal, while soulless corporations do not. Make a man famous and you make his creation famous." The founder who designed the thing and can answer for every detail is the strongest spokesman it will ever have.

**Stay close to the buyer.** As a young man Hopkins stood under torchlight for hours watching street pitchmen, noting which appeals lit faces and which made people walk. Study salesmen, canvassers, even fakers. Distance from the buyer is the enemy of good copy.

**Effort.** "The advertising man who spares the midnight oil will never get very far." Hopkins attributed his record to hours, not brains, and to making every mistake once. He earned it all "at a typewriter, which I operated myself without a clerk or a secretary."

## Headline patterns

Each pattern with examples. Real examples are marked; the rest are illustrative and any numbers in them are placeholders to be replaced with the client's true figures.

**The specific promise.** The benefit, with a number that proves someone measured.
- "1,000 songs in your pocket." *(real, Apple)*
- "A 78-second shave." *(real, per Hopkins)*
- "Your invoices out the day the work is done. Not eleven days later."

**The untold commonplace.** The thing every maker does and nobody says.
- "Every bottle is washed four times before it's filled." *(Schlitz-style)*
- "We throw away one roast in eight. Here's what that tastes like."
- "Our inspectors reject 3% of what our suppliers send. Most brands accept it."

**Name the reader.** Pick them out of the crowd.
- "For the freelancer who has never once sent an invoice on time."
- "If you run a restaurant with under 40 seats, this is priced for you."
- "Landlords: your 2026 tax bill is decided by what you do before Friday."

**The honest confession.** Powers' bankruptcy ad, in modern form.
- "We over-ordered winter stock. Everything is 30% off until it's gone."
- "This is the plainest coat we make. Here's why it costs more than the fancy one."
- "We lost our biggest customer. Their unused hours are yours at cost."

**Curiosity with a guaranteed payoff.** Open a gap the body must close.
- "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise comes from the electric clock." *(real, Ogilvy)*
- "He told a brewery to publish its cleaning schedule. It went from 5th to 1st."
- "The one line in a 300-page manual that sold a million dollars of toothpaste."

**Reward, not prevention.** Turn the fear into the wanted outcome.
- Prevention: "Stop losing customers at checkout." → Reward: "Three more sales out of every ten people who reach your checkout."
- Prevention: "Don't let your data get breached." → Reward: "Pass the security review your biggest prospect just sent you."

**The limited group.** Belonging, when the limit is real.
- "Open to the first 200 shops in Pune. Then we close it for a year."
- "For paying members only: the pricing sheet we don't publish."

Weak patterns to avoid: the pun that must be decoded, the mystery that reveals nothing ("Something big is coming"), the boast ("The world's best X"), the topic label ("Our approach to quality"), the question the reader can answer "no" to ("Ready to transform your workflow?").

## Worked example (hypothetical)

**Brief:** Northfield Roasters, a small coffee roaster, wants a landing page. Founder's draft headline: "Premium specialty coffee, roasted with passion."

**Step 1 — Research.** Questions to the founder turn up: beans come from three farms the founder has visited; every batch is cupped and 1 in 8 is rejected and sold as compost-grade; beans ship within 48 hours of roasting; the founder, Meera, roasts every batch herself; average supermarket coffee is 6–9 months past roast. (All figures are hypothetical; in a real job every one would be confirmed.)

**Step 2 — Appeal.** Not "premium" or "passion." The buyer gets coffee that tastes like it was roasted this week, because it was. The untold commonplace is the rejection rate and the 48-hour shipping.

**Step 3 — Headlines.** Candidates (first five are about the company; they get better):
1. Premium specialty coffee, roasted with passion. *(boast, generic)*
2. Northfield Roasters: small batch, big flavor. *(slogan, says nothing)*
3. Coffee roasted by hand in Northfield. *(about us)*
4. Taste the difference freshness makes. *(platitude)*
5. Specialty coffee, delivered. *(category)*
6. Roasted Tuesday. At your door Thursday.
7. The coffee in your supermarket was roasted 6 to 9 months ago. Ours, this week.
8. We throw away one batch in eight. You drink the seven.
9. Meera roasts every bag herself. Here's what she rejects.
10. Coffee shipped within 48 hours of roasting, or it doesn't ship.
11. For people who've noticed their coffee stopped tasting like anything.
12. Three farms. One roaster. Forty-eight hours.

Pick: **#7** as headline, **#8** as subhead. #7 names the reader's situation (they buy supermarket coffee), makes a specific comparison, and creates curiosity the body pays off. #8 is the untold commonplace.

**Step 4 — Body (excerpt).**

> Coffee is a fresh food. Roasted beans peak in the first two weeks and fade after that, and most bags on a supermarket shelf were roasted six to nine months before you bought them. That's why your coffee stopped tasting like anything. It isn't you.
>
> Here is what we do about it. I roast every batch myself, in small drums, on Mondays and Thursdays. I cup each one before it's bagged. About one in eight doesn't meet the standard and goes to a compost supplier instead of to you. The seven that pass are bagged, labeled with the roast date, and shipped within 48 hours. If a bag would miss that window, it doesn't ship.
>
> The beans come from three farms in [region], [region], and [region]. I've visited all three. [One sentence on each, from the founder.]
>
> If you'd like to taste the difference before committing, reply to this page with your address and I'll send a 100g sample of whichever roast you choose. Then decide.

**Step 5 — Selfishness cut.** Deleted from the founder's draft: "premium," "passion," "the best coffee you'll ever taste," "don't settle for less," and a paragraph on the roastery's 2019 founding.

**Step 6 — Check.** Salesman test: yes, this is what Meera would say to a customer at a market stall. Appeal in headline: yes. One person: yes ("your coffee stopped tasting like anything"). We/you count: you wins. Specifics: 6–9 months, 1 in 8, 48 hours, three farms. Pains taken: yes. Reward: coffee that tastes like something. Boasts: none. Claims sourced: from founder interview; farm details left as placeholders. Easy action: a sample, requested by reply.

## Before and after gallery

**Landing-page hero (SaaS)**
- Before: "The all-in-one platform trusted by industry leaders to streamline workflows."
- After: "Your invoices go out the day the work is done. Not eleven days later, which is the current average for teams your size."

**Cold email subject line**
- Before: "Quick question about your marketing"
- After: "Your checkout page is losing about 1 in 4 buyers at step 3"

**Cold email body (opening)**
- Before: "Hi Priya, I'm reaching out from Acme, a leading provider of analytics solutions for e-commerce brands. We help companies like yours..."
- After: "Priya, I ran your checkout through a browser on a slow phone connection. Step 3 takes 11 seconds to load; the industry drop-off at that delay is about 25%. Here's the 40-second screen recording. If it's useful, I can show you the two changes that usually fix it, no call needed."

**Product description (physical goods)**
- Before: "Our premium leather wallet combines timeless style with unmatched craftsmanship."
- After: "This wallet is cut from the middle of the hide, where the grain is tightest. We use about 40% of each hide and sell the rest to belt makers. Each one is stitched by one person, start to finish, and signed inside. Carry it for ten years; if a stitch goes, send it back and the same person repairs it."

**Billboard**
- Before: "Acme Cloud. Powering the future of business."
- After: "Deploys in 40 seconds. Ask the 900 teams who timed it."

**Article title**
- Before: "Lessons in advertising from Claude Hopkins"
- After: "He told a brewery to publish its cleaning schedule. It went from 5th to 1st."

**Podcast / video clip title**
- Before: "Founder discusses hiring philosophy"
- After: "Why she rejects any candidate who's never been fired"

**Social post**
- Before: "Excited to announce the launch of our new feature! Check it out and let us know what you think 🚀"
- After: "Until today, exporting a report took 14 clicks. We counted. Now it's one. Here's the 20-second video of the old way next to the new way."

**About page**
- Before: "Founded in 2015, Acme has grown to become a trusted partner for over 500 clients worldwide. Our mission is to deliver excellence."
- After: "I started Acme after my third accountant lost my receipts. Everything here is built for the freelancer who does their own books at 11pm on a Sunday, because for six years that was me."

**Sale announcement**
- Before: "Don't miss our biggest sale of the year! Insist on genuine Acme."
- After: "We over-ordered winter stock. Everything is 30% off through Sunday. Here's what's actually worth buying at that price, and what isn't."

**Newsletter opening**
- Before: "In this week's issue we explore the important topic of customer retention."
- After: "A subscription company I advise found that customers who used the product on day 2 stayed four times longer than those who didn't. Nothing else predicted retention nearly as well. Here's what they changed about day 1."

## Writing the sentence

Hopkins wrote the way he sold: plainly, to one person, repeating what mattered. Apply this to articles and essays as much as to ads.

- **One idea per sentence.** Short declaratives. Let the reader breathe.
- **Concrete nouns, active verbs.** "Beer is cooled in filtered air" beats "our commitment to purity."
- **Say the thing, then say it again differently.** Hopkins repeated his six ideas dozens of times across two books, with different words and different stories. The reader remembers what you made unforgettable, not what you mentioned once.
- **Delete platitudes.** If a sentence could appear in a competitor's material unchanged, cut it or make it specific.
- **Prefer the story to the assertion.** "Tell the truth" is a rule. The bankrupt clothier's ad is a story you'll never forget.
- **Never mislead, even by omission.** The truth told well is the most durable advantage in the field, partly because so few use it.
- **Earn attention before you educate.** Entertain, then inform, then inspire. Nobody reads what bores them, however useful.
- **Work as play.** The twentieth headline is better than the first, and only someone enjoying the twentieth will write it.

## Adapting to format

| Format | Headline | Body | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Print/social ad, billboard | The appeal in ≤8 words | 1–3 specific proofs | One next step |
| Landing / sales page | Promise + who it's for | Full case: how it works, pains taken, numbers, proof, objections | Trial, demo, or sample; low effort to start |
| Sales / outreach email | Subject = a benefit or a specific, never a greeting | Written to one named situation; service before ask | A single reply-able question |
| Product description | The one thing it does for the user | How it's made and why that matters | Clear buy or try |
| Article / essay / newsletter | The most useful or surprising idea, not the topic | Story-led, specific, repeats the core idea in new forms | What the reader can do with it |
| Podcast / video clip title | The single idea the clip proves, in a listener's words | (the clip is the body) | — |
| About page | Who it's for and why it exists | The founder, first person, the pains taken | Contact or try |

## Self-check (run on every draft)

1. **Salesman test:** would a good salesman say this to a half-sold buyer in front of him? Anything he wouldn't say, cut.
2. **Appeal in the headline?** Could a reader who reads only the headline know what they'd get?
3. **One person?** Or "customers," "businesses," "everyone"?
4. **We vs. you.** Count the sentences. The reader should win.
5. **At least one specific** a competitor couldn't paste in unchanged?
6. **Pains taken** told, or just quality claimed?
7. **Reward or prevention?** Beauty or decay?
8. **Any boast, "buy my brand," or platitude left?** Delete.
9. **Every claim true and sourced** from the user's material? Placeholders marked as placeholders?
10. **Easy action** at the end that asks for a small effort?

When critiquing someone else's copy, score it against these ten, quote the offending lines, and rewrite each one rather than describing the problem in the abstract.
