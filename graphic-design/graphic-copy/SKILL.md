# Graphic copy

Copy in a graphic is read faster and judged harder than prose. The [Claudisms banlist](../../editorial/claudisms/SKILL.md) applies in full. These rules add what the user has flagged specifically in slides and cards.

## 1. No invented vignettes

Seen: a panel that turned "feedback at night, changed by breakfast" into a three-row timetable, "Their night 22:00 / Our morning 08:00 / Their breakfast 08:30". The user called it an AI-ism: talking about their breakfast and their night is not something you're supposed to talk about.

Apply:

- Do not stage a little scene to dramatise a plain fact: times of day, meals, a diary rhythm, characters going about their morning.
- Do not invent specifics the source does not contain. Clock times, counts, and durations that were not in the article are fabrication, even when they feel illustrative. Never invent evidence to fit a design: if the layout wants a number the source does not have, change the layout, not the facts. This is the same rule as in [Hopkins copywriting](../../copywriting/hopkins-copywriting/SKILL.md): never invent a statistic, process detail, testimonial or claim.
- State the fact in the article's own words. "Feedback comes in overnight. The new version is live before their morning." One line, no panel.

## 2. A panel needs data

Rows, cells and bars exist to show numbers or comparisons the source actually contains. If the article has no numbers for a section, do not build a panel to hold prose fragments. Write one sentence, or leave the card as a title and a body.

## 3. Titles are claims

Card and slide titles state something arguable in the article's voice: "Mockups existed because code was expensive", not "Background" or "The old process". Read only the titles: they should carry the argument in order.

## 4. Keep the article's register, but write from extracted facts

The graphic keeps the author's voice: plain, direct, no cutesy paraphrase. It does not lift the author's sentences as slide copy (see rule 9). Where a fact needs a sentence, write the shortest factual sentence in that register.

## 5. One idea per surface

One idea per card, one claim per slide. If a card needs two panels and three paragraphs, it is two cards. If a slide needs a sub-list of six, the six items are the slide.

## 6. Labels that do navigational work only

Mono eyebrows, footers and tags stay if they orient the reader (page numbers, section names, source URL). Cut any label that comments on the content ("what changed", "the whole difference") instead of naming it.

## 7. No kicker copy

A hard rule from the user: no small line of text above a heading, anywhere. Do not write eyebrows, kickers or section labels for slides. Put the idea in the heading or cut it. Turn a label plus a one-word heading into a question or a claim: "But what about taste?" rather than "WHAT YOU LOSE" over "TASTE."

## 8. Closers that lean on a person

Seen: "Dana's title doesn't say design." (Dana being a colleague mentioned in the article) used as a closing line in every output. The user asked for it to be removed everywhere: it sounds off. Do not end on a quip about a named colleague's job title or role. End on the claim.

## 9. Carousels are factual; narrative devices stay in the article

Seen: slides built on the article's haunted-house list (the milk goes bad, the cat drags in a dead bird), a giant "1826" for the string quartet, "You're the fifth example in somebody else's article". The user: that is a narrative tool used within the article, not something you use in a carousel. Carousels are tight and short and deliver facts. An article can lead with narrative; a carousel leads with information.

Apply:

- Read the article for its information value: numbers, mechanisms, timelines, comparisons, the moves it recommends, the evidence it cites. Build the slides from that.
- Do not lift the author's lines, jokes, images or metaphors into slides. Understand the article, extract, then write.
- If a section of the article is only narrative, its slide states the fact the narrative was illustrating (the symptoms of decline read as isolated events) or it has no slide.

## 10. The hook delivers information

Seen: "The Great Reset of agency work. Your retainer hasn't noticed." The user: what does that mean? It delivers no information, it just opens questions. Not a hook.

Apply: the first slide states the main fact in full: what changed, by how much, and what it means for the reader. "Agency deliverables now cost 10x to 100x less to produce. Most retainers are still priced at the old cost." A teaser that withholds the fact is not a hook.

## 11. One fact per giant figure

A giant number is only for a figure that carries information on its own (10x to 100x, three days to four hours, $50K to $20K). A year or a count that only makes sense inside a story (1826) is not a slide figure.

## 12. Headings say the thing, in literal words

Seen: "Four habits decide whether you get the hit." The user: what hit, what habits, what decision? It opens questions instead of stating the fact. Also "the cache is private to your organisation and lives 30 minutes" and "predicted outputs stopped at the GPT-4.1 family."

Apply:

- A heading can be long. It cannot be oblique. Write the fact the section proves: "A prompt gets cached when its start stays the same across requests and it is longer than 1,024 tokens."
- No metaphor where a literal phrase exists: not "lives", "stopped at", "earns", "hit", "dial". The Claudisms banlist has a section on mannered prose; run it on every heading and row title.
- If the heading needs the reader to open the section to learn what it means, rewrite it.

## Checklist

1. Any scene, timetable or time-of-day staging? Cut it.
2. Any number, time or count not in the source? Cut it.
3. Any panel without source data behind it? Turn it into a sentence.
4. Titles read in order as the argument?
5. Claudisms pass done on every string, including chips, tags and captions?
6. Any kicker or label line above a heading? Cut it.
7. Any closing quip about a named person? Cut it.
8. Any narrative device lifted from the article (scene, list of omens, joke, metaphor)? Replace with the fact it illustrates.
9. Does the first slide state the main fact, not tease it?
10. Is every giant figure a fact that stands alone?
11. Does every heading state its fact in literal words, with no metaphor and no open question?
